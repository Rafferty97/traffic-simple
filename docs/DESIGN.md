# Traffic Simulator — Master Design

This is the canonical design: how the finished simulator works, both what is built and what is planned. Its models are described in their own docs, linked from Models below. Keep it current, changing it in the same commit as the code it describes, and ask Alexander before changing its goals.

## Goals and working model

The goal is city and freeway traffic that looks realistic at 60 fps, built as simply and fast as possible. Where visual realism and aggregate accuracy conflict, realism wins.

**Goals**

- Believable motion through lane changes, merges, diverges and junctions, including hook turns
- Trams, as in Melbourne: on tracks in the road or on their own, stopping at their stops, with cars stopping behind a tram at a stop in the road
- Intersections controlled as real ones are: signals, give-way and stop signs, all-way stops and keep-clear zones, with turning traffic giving way as drivers do
- Vehicles that find their way to destinations, changing lanes in good time
- 60 fps rendering with a simple, fast core: a step for 10,000 vehicles well inside a frame (**Suggestion:** under 2 ms on a laptop, as the benchmarks measure it)
- Traffic flow that doesn't break down in ways real traffic wouldn't
- Algorithms made visible in a debug view

**Non-goals**

- Calibrated aggregate outputs for policy work (SUMO and VISSIM already do this)
- Building networks from real-world data: networks are hand-crafted or procedural
- Demand modelling inside the core simulation
- Pedestrians, cyclists, passengers and parking
- Optimising signals: plans are fixed-time, with actuated control left to a module boundary
- Preventing gridlock: it is allowed, and fun to watch

**Working model**

- Claude does all the typing, and keeps the code tidy and modular as it goes. Alexander reviews as needed, owns the goals, and is asked about choices of taste or of the project's objectives.
- New dependencies, non-trivial `unsafe`, and allocation or data-layout changes in the simulation loop are proposed to Alexander first.
- "Swappable" means a clean module boundary, replaced by editing the module, not by traits or runtime dispatch.
- Where it can, the simulation follows established technique (SUMO, IDM and LMRS literature, game-engine practice). Its deliberate departures are a rear axle trailing in world space rather than lane coordinates as the whole truth, how vehicles find their leaders across lanes (see [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md)), the junction backstop and merges; each is marked **Off the beaten path**.
- In these docs, unmarked statements are decisions. **Suggestion** marks proposals that haven't been explicitly confirmed yet. **Planned** marks designed parts not yet built. Open items are collected in [Known issues](known-issues.md).
- This doc is the architecture and the core; each model has its own doc, under `docs/models/` (see Models), and multi-lane car following has [its own](multilane-car-following.md).

## Workspace and crates

One Rust workspace. The simulation crate depends on nothing else in the workspace; everything else depends on it.

| Crate                     | Owns                                                                                                      | Depends on       |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| `sim`                     | Simulation state, models, update pipeline, public API, and the `SimNetwork` type it consumes             | —                |
| `network`                 | Road-level network format and scenarios; compiles them to a `SimNetwork` and to render geometry          | `sim`            |
| `ui`                      | egui app with a pretty view and a debug view                                                              | `sim`, `network` |
| `demand` (**Planned**)    | Spawners, origin–destination demand and vehicle mixes, kept outside the core                              | `sim`            |
| `validation` (**Planned**) | A headless harness measuring aggregate behaviour against published values                               | `sim`, `network`, `demand` |

`sim` defines `SimNetwork` and `network` produces it, so the dependency graph is one-way and the core never sees authoring concepts like multi-lane roads.

Inside `sim`, the core is grouped by what each part does:

- `simulation`: the `Simulation` and its public API; `simulation::step`, the whole update pipeline in one file, each phase reading the state, working out what every vehicle decides and applying it; `simulation::accel`, how one vehicle's acceleration is put together from its terms; and `simulation::state`, the state and what is derived from it.
- `network`: the static world, `SimNetwork`, its lanes and their links, the barriers and signal plans controlling them, its junctions and their conflicts, and lane geometry.
- `motion`: moving one vehicle along its course: the front axle along its path and the rear axle trailing it, a lane change's course (lateral motion is core, and outside a lane change vehicles follow their lanes' centrelines), and how far ahead its path reaches.
- `following`: multi-lane car following, as [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md) has it, a submodule for each part: the occupancy records, joining lanes, the overlaps found when the simulation is built, classifying records, the leader search and the pass check.
- `query`: the other searches of the traffic, for the models and the core: searching back for a follower, each lane's speeds ahead, and a vehicle's acceleration along other lanes.
- `barrier` (commitment, passing and release), `junction` (the backstop) and `merge`: the core's mechanisms for ordering vehicles.

The models are apart from it, each replaced by editing it: `acceleration`, the longitudinal model, which the core asks for terms throughout, so a module boundary that will never be dispatched at runtime; `lane_change`, LMRS, its measurements, decisions and acceleration terms; `gap_acceptance`, when the head at a barrier commits, from gaps, room beyond and arrival order; `routing`, choices at forks and the lane changes a route needs; `signal`; and `driver`, each driver's traits and the state they vary with.

## Network pipeline

One road-level network description compiles into two products: a `SimNetwork` for the simulation and render geometry for the UI.

- **Source of truth:** built by procedural generators (`scenarios`) or by hand. Real-world import is out of scope. The format will change as it grows.
- **`SimNetwork` output:** lanes as polylines, their successors, neighbours with lane-change permissions, which side drivers keep to, how lanes that end are left, the road each is part of, for routing, junctions with their conflicts, signal plans, and barriers: signals, give-way and stop lines, all-way stops and keep-clear zones. **Planned:** tram stops.
- **Render output:** road and junction surfaces, lane markings, stop lines, turn arrows and keep-clear zones.
- **Precision:** every lane point and half-width is snapped to a 1 mm grid as the lane is built, so a network serializes exactly as short text. Two grid points under 2 mm apart are the same point: snapping leaves two copies of one at most √2 mm apart, and the next distance between grid points is 2 mm. That is how lane ends are matched and checked, with no other tolerance.

The `network` crate describes networks at road level, as OpenDRIVE and SUMO's edge/lane/connection model do, and expands them to bare lanes during compilation. That is how people think about roads, and it keeps the sim free of road concepts.

- **Roads:** a reference line, a start pose followed by pieces (lines, arcs and cubic Béziers) that each continue from the last, and lanes beside it, each with an offset and a width that are constant or ease (smoothstep) from one value to another over a stretch, and a direction along the road. Clothoids can come later.
- **Sampling:** curves, junction connectors included, are sampled by tolerance, not at a fixed spacing: at most 1 cm of sagitta (`c² / 8R`) per vertex, and 15° of turn as a backstop, since vehicles round each vertex (see Vehicle kinematics), and where an offset or width eases, enough vertices that it strays at most 1 cm from the smoothstep between them. The vertex next to each end of a road goes on the tangent there, as far out as stays within 1 cm of the curve, so lanes end square across the road.
- **Lanes from one polyline:** a road's lanes are offset from one sampled reference polyline by its miters, so neighbouring lanes start and end square across the road, side by side, as `validate` requires, and meet the next road's lanes exactly; but at a skewed stop line, each lane coming in ends at its own (see Arms).
- **Neighbours:** lanes of a road running side by side the same way and keeping their distance; lanes easing together, at a lane drop, or apart, are not. Each lane says whether vehicles may change into its neighbour on the left and on the right, as SUMO's `changeLeft` and `changeRight` do; both by default.
- **Lane use** (**Planned**): each lane says which vehicle classes may use it, as SUMO's `allow` and `disallow` do: all but trams by default, trams only on tram tracks, and buses only on bus lanes. A road's tram tracks are lanes like any other, either their own or shared with general traffic.
- **Roads for routing:** compilation gives each lane the road it is part of, an opaque `RoadId`: each way of a road is one, and each way through an intersection from one road into another, all its connectors, is another. Lanes of one road are equal for finding a way, as a road's lanes are to a driver choosing a route, as SUMO routes by edges and then picks among an edge's lanes; only the lane changes they need tell them apart (see [Routing](models/routing.md)).
- **Keeping to a side:** the road network says which side drivers keep to on roads above a speed limit: in Australia, the left, above 80 km/h. Compilation marks those roads' lanes.
- **Lanes that end by merging,** as an acceleration lane does, say how drivers leave them: at the merge, taking turns (a zipper), or along the way (early). The ramps scenario offers both.
- **Joins:** a road's end joins the next road's start, and lanes connect wherever one ends where the other starts: two meeting one merge, one meeting two diverge, with nothing declared. Merging road lanes are not a junction: they are a merge (see Merges).
- **Intersections:** each lists which lanes connect to which, and each connection becomes a connector from one lane's end to the other's start: along the stretch of its road the intersection takes before it (see Arms), then a quintic Bézier along their directions, with no curvature at either end: the cubic with its control points a third of the way across, raised to a quintic, its next control points moved onto its ends' lines, gentler than that cubic at its sharpest and a few percent longer; then along the other road's stretch, in segments no longer than 1 m so that its width can narrow along them, and its speed limit lowered for its tightest bend (3.5 m/s² sideways, as AASHTO's side friction factors for intersection curves at 15–25 km/h have it, so 15 to 20 km/h round the crossroads' turns, and at least 3 m/s). A connector's width is the network's to set, as one width. By default it is the swept path of the widest vehicle along it, as road design sizes turning roadways from a design vehicle's swept path, plus the room one vehicle needs to pass another (0.5 m) on each side: driven along it as the simulation drives it, its front axle on the path and its rear axle trailing, at each vertex its front axle reaches each corner is projected onto the centreline, and each vertex is as wide each side as the furthest corner there, never less than half the vehicle's width, then tapered so that it never widens faster than 1 m in 4 m along, so the envelope doesn't step out where a bend begins. A straight connector is then a car's width plus a metre, narrower than a road lane, so connectors side by side at a mouth don't touch and conflict there, and a turner's first conflict is where its way really meets another's; on a bend the inner side widens where the rear axle cuts inside the front's course, about 0.5 m on a tight left turn, and the outer side a little where the front corner swings out, so a vehicle's body stays within its lanes, as the multi-lane following model needs. An intersection's connectors are one junction. A far-side turn first runs straight on along its approach for the intersection's `far_side_straight` before its curve begins, as drivers turning across traffic pull into the intersection to wait: 3 m in the crossroads scenarios.
- **Render output:** each road's surface between its outermost edges; lane markings, dashed between lanes going the same way, a centre line between opposite ones, and edge lines; and each intersection's surface, the mouths of its roads joined by cubic Bézier curbs.
- **Arms:** how each road meets an intersection, its arm, is part of the network: how far back from the road's end its stop line crosses the reference line, the stop line's skew from square across the road, and its run out, how far into the road turns into it may finish. Each of the road's lanes coming in ends square across itself where the stop line crosses its centre, so at a skewed line each ends at its own, and the stop line's barrier, carrying the line, is at the very start of each connector: a vehicle stopping by the line, its nearest corner just short of it, has its front short of its lane's end, never in the junction before it commits, and its lanes going out start the run out from its end; those stretches belong to the intersection's connectors, so a vehicle's body sweeping across them as it turns is in the junction, where its conflicts are found, not on a road lane where nobody would see it. A turn into a lane of a road other than the nearest on its side drifts across the run out to it, as drivers turning into a far lane do; every other connector into the road runs along the run out to its lane. Compilation fails if a vehicle's body would sweep onto a road lane the connector doesn't join, or, past the connector's end while its nose is still turning, onto another connector: its sweep is the widest vehicle's, as connectors are sized, run on past each end from its front bumper reaching the start to its rear bumper leaving the end, plus 5 cm for the rounding of its course's corners and the steps its rear axle trails in. `fit_arms` sets stop lines back and lengthens run outs until it compiles, each by as far as a sweep reaches along the lane, as road design sets stop lines back to clear turning vehicles' swept paths. With the front axle on the path, the rear cuts inside a turn, toward the kerb or its own side, rather than the front swinging out over the lanes beside, so the square crossroads scenarios need no setting back, and the oblique ones only what their skew needs.
- **Stop lines:** each lane coming into an intersection gets one along its arm's stop line, across the lane, 300 mm wide as AS 1742.2 has it, set back half that so its far edge is on the line. At signals, each connector from the lane gets its signal barrier where its lane's edge first meets the line, with the line itself if it is skewed (see Barriers and signals).
- **Turn arrows:** each lane coming into an intersection gets an arrow 8 m back from where its centre crosses its stop line for the ways its connections go, as SUMO classes a link's direction by its angle: straight on within 30° either way, otherwise left or right. Its ways are the connections' turn classes (see Intersection control). It's one of seven glyphs, every mix of left, straight and right, drawn in `ui/assets/lane-glyphs.svg` and converted once to triangles in `ui/src/glyphs.rs` by `ui/assets/convert-glyphs.mjs`, so the UI needs no SVG support. None is painted on a road shorter than 16 m.

**Intersection control** (partly **Planned**) turns how an intersection is controlled into barriers, priorities and signal plans, as SUMO's netconvert derives right of way and traffic-light logic from road priorities and turn directions, simplified.

- **Turn classes:** compilation classes each connection as straight on, within 30° either way, or a turn, and reports it beside the connectors; a turn is near-side, toward the side traffic drives on, which the network says (the left by default, as in Australia), or far-side, across oncoming traffic. Arrows, priorities and signal movements all use it. **Built:** the classes and the arrows reading them.
- **Control:** an intersection is controlled by priority, naming the roads that have it; as an all-way stop; or by signals, with a fixed-time plan. An approach without priority has a give-way or a stop sign, give way by default. Uncontrolled, it has only the junction backstop. A far-side turn from a road without priority takes one gap from its line, across all the traffic it gives way to at once. **Built:** uncontrolled, priority, all-way stops, and signals, with their priority levels and unprotected turns' waiting lines. **Planned:** far-side turns from a road without priority that cross one carriageway, wait in the median, then take a gap in the other, where a divided road leaves room for them.
- **Priority levels:** compilation gives each connector a level, the higher going first: straight on or near-side from a priority road, 3; far-side from one, 2; straight on or near-side from any other road, 1; far-side from any other, 0. Signalised intersections treat every road as a priority road, so far-side turns still give way to oncoming traffic when both have a green. Levels are compilation's alone: from them it works out the lanes each barrier gives way to (see Barriers from control), as SUMO's netconvert derives right of way from road priorities, and the simulation has no levels, and no level of its own for a red light. Barriers read the lanes they give way to, and lights only say go or stop.
- **Barriers from control:** each connector from an approach without priority starts with a give-way or stop barrier; every connector of an all-way stop with a stop barrier; and every connector of a signalised intersection with a signal barrier for its movement. A far-side turn that conflicts with a connector of a higher level that can be moving at the same time (at a priority intersection, always; at signals, if their movements share a phase) also gets a give-way barrier inside the junction at the start of its first such conflict. There it waits for a gap, clear of the traffic behind it, as drivers turning across do. The barrier sits on the connector, partway along it, so no stretch of the connector before it meets the traffic it gives way to. Each barrier carries the lanes it gives way to, worked out once by compilation, so the simulation derives nothing at runtime. A far-side turn's barrier inside the junction gives way only to its oncoming traffic: every lane of the connectors of a higher level green with it. A give-way or stop barrier at the line of an approach without priority gives way to every lane of the connectors of a higher level whose lanes conflict with its own, the waiting turners' connectors among them, and takes turns with every lane of the connectors of the same level from other such lines whose lanes conflict with its own: their vehicles count as priority traffic, unless it came to rest at its line before them (see [Gap acceptance](models/gap-acceptance.md)). An all-way stop is stop lines giving way to nothing of higher priority, each taking turns with every connector whose lanes conflict with its own, so arrival order decides. Priority levels already have turners give way to traffic going straight on, so outside all-way stops equal levels meet at lines only rarely, as opposite far-side turns from minor roads whose paths cross do. A signal barrier gives way to the turners waiting inside on movements that never share a phase with it whose way past their waiting lines crosses its own, carrying their connectors, as drivers give way to vehicles in an intersection: driver behaviour, which the junction doesn't rely on (see Queue invariants).
- **Signal plans** (**Built**)**:** a plan is a list of phases, each the movements green in it and how long for. A movement is an approach and a turn class, whose connectors go and stop together; connectors only trams may use are movements of their own, so trams can have their own phases. Two movements conflict if any of their connectors do, except a far-side turn and the traffic it gives way to, which may share a phase as an unprotected turn (a filter turn, in Australian usage), the turner giving way to the oncoming traffic. A movement's amber time is ITE's, a second's reaction plus `v / (2 · 3 m/s²)` at its approach's limit, and its clearance the time to cross its longest connector and a car's length at that limit.
- **Hook turns:** at signals, an approach may make its far-side turns hook turns, as in Melbourne, keeping turning traffic out of the tram tracks in the middle of the road. Each connector leaves from the near-side lane and runs straight on to a waiting line, then turns. The waiting line comes from the geometry of the road on the near side, whose traffic will follow the turner into the road turned into: from the kerb-side corner of that road's stop line, offset by a fixed padding along the road and across it, a stop line perpendicular to that one, across the turner's way. There a signal barrier for the movement of the road turned into holds the turner until that road's light turns green, when it turns ahead of that road's traffic.
- **Keep-clear zones:** a road may mark stretches keep clear for its traffic going one way, as a box is painted across a side road's mouth. Each becomes a barrier at its start on every lane across it, going that way, with the zone's length along the lane, and the reference line gets a vertex at each end, so the zone is square across the road. It is painted as a yellow box, hatched at 45°.
- **Exits and entries:** compilation reports each road end's lanes into and out of the network, so demand can place vehicles and name destinations by road.

Generating junction geometry is the hardest part of this crate. SUMO's netconvert is largely heuristics for it; procedural networks keep it tractable.

## Lane model and geometry

The simulation has no roads, only lanes. Lanes may overlap, merge, diverge or cross, and lane changes may be permitted between them.

- **Geometry:** each lane is a polyline with arc length s and lateral offset t. Each vertex has its own left and right half-widths, so a lane can taper and its centreline needn't be centred; between vertices they vary linearly in s, as OpenDRIVE gives lane width as a function of s. A projection from world to lane coordinates starts from a hint, the last one's result with its segment, and scans from there, so it is close to O(1) for a vehicle that has moved a little.
- **Serialized geometry:** a lane's geometry serializes as a string of vertices in whole millimetres, each `x,y,left,right`; with three values the half-widths are equal, and with two they are the previous vertex's: `0,0,1750 90000,0 100000,2000,2000,1750`. Being on the grid, it reads back exactly.
- **Module boundary:** a lane's geometry, `LaneGeometry`, is its own type in its own module; the `Lane` holds it with its speed limit, keep side, how it is left if it ends, and links, which only the network sets.
- **Relationships:** successors and predecessors (end-to-start connections), kept in order from left to right; left and right neighbours, at most one each side, with permission per ordered pair, since a change may be allowed one way only, and the stretch of each lane over which the two run side by side; and overlaps, found when the simulation is built (see [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md)).
- **Barriers:** each lane has its barriers, sorted by s, each with the lanes it gives way to, if any, in a table the network keeps.
- **Lane sections:** adjacent lanes run side by side together, like the lanes of a SUMO edge or an OpenDRIVE lane section: each neighbour link holds the stretch of its lane the two are beside each other over, square across both at either end, so the neighbour of a lane's successor is the successor of its neighbour wherever they run side by side to their ends, which lets a vehicle map its path to the lanes beside it. Lanes need only end together where a change may run beyond the stretch: coming into an intersection at a skewed stop line, each ends at its own line, and no change runs past where the first of them ends. `SimNetwork::validate` checks this, with mutual neighbours and successors starting where their lane ends.

**Off the beaten path:** SUMO, VISSIM and Aimsun treat lane position as the source of truth and derive world position from it. Here the truth is half each: a vehicle's front axle is placed by how far along its path it is, in lane coordinates, as theirs is, and its rear axle trails it in world space, its position the only world coordinate kept, since where it is depends on the course the front has taken, not only on where the front is now; the rear axle's lane coordinates are derived from it by projection. The reason is realistic motion through lane changes, merges and junctions: the front holds its lane's line exactly, so a body's sweep is known in advance, and the rear cuts inside a bend as a car's does (see Vehicle kinematics).

## Simulation state and data layout

All vehicles live in one global `SlotMap`, an array of structs, holding what the core needs. Each model owns its per-vehicle state, in a `SecondaryMap` keyed by vehicle that only it reads or writes, so the boundary between it and the core is in the data too: the drivers', the longitudinal model's, the lane-change model's and the routes. The core tells each model when a vehicle is inserted and removed, and asks it through methods for what it needs, so a model could later become a boxed trait holding its own map. Against keeping it all on the vehicle, that costs about 2% a step on the busy three-lane ring, where every vehicle weighs up lane changes, and nothing measurable on the city grid. Each lane keeps a list of occupant records sorted by s, and a vehicle appears in every lane it occupies. If profiling shows frame-rate work is memory-bound, the hot fields could split into a struct of arrays (**Suggestion**).

The state is one struct, `SimState`, holding everything that can't be rebuilt from the rest: the network, the vehicles, each model's per-vehicle state, the occupant lists, the junction queues, speed caps, the vehicles queued for removal, the step count, time and config, each signal controller's state, and the destinations registered. It is exactly what a freeze frame saves. What can be rebuilt is derived from it when a simulation is built or restored, and kept apart: the overlaps between lanes, the merges, how far beyond each lane the paths through it reach (see Searching back), and each destination's route table.

**Vehicle state**

- World pose: the rear axle's position and the heading as a unit vector, so moving needs no trigonometry; how far along its path its front axle is; speed; and the curvature of its path, for the inspector
- Acceleration, held between longitudinal updates
- Path: the lane it follows and the lanes it will take next, as far as its leader search, path tracking and lane change look, and as far as it would go in 15 s at each lane's speed limit, however short the lanes. The time is at the limits, not its speed, so a slow vehicle in a queue still knows where it is going, and merges and junctions ahead know of it; its leader search stays within its horizon however far the path reaches. A path ending inside a junction is extended until it is out of it, so a vehicle in a junction's queue always knows its way through. It is a `Vec`. Beside it, how far it reaches: where it ends along its first lane, and where too little of it would be left ahead at the limits, so each step checks two numbers rather than adding up its lanes, and extends it only when it may be short.
- Commitment: how many of the barriers ahead of its rear axle on its path it has committed to crossing, a `u8`, and the step at which it came to rest at a stop line, if it has (see Barriers and signals)
- Lane-change intent: nothing, wanting to change to a side, or indicating to it and since when, as the lane-change model last decided (`LaneChangeIntent`)
- Held up: the vehicle behind that it holds up most, as of the last longitudinal update, with that vehicle's acceleration from every other term: the core's output, which the lane-change model's courtesy incentive reads
- The old side and lane change in progress
- Class and static attributes (`VehicleSpec`): its body and the longitudinal model's parameters, with a preset for each class (see Vehicle classes); its driver's traits are the driver model's

Each model's, beside it:

- **Driver model:** its driver's traits, its acceleration noise now, how far short of the barrier it is heading for it will stop, drawn for each, and its own random generator's state, for the noise and those gaps (see [the driver model](models/driver.md))
- **Longitudinal model:** whether it is finishing a stop (see [the longitudinal model](models/longitudinal.md), Finishing a stop)
- **Lane-change model:** its desire, cooldown and the ghost it makes room for, and its driver's courtesy, politeness and assertiveness, copied at insertion
- **Routing:** its route, how it chooses its way at forks, with any state that needs (see [Routing](models/routing.md))

**Per-lane occupancy**

- Each record is `(vehicle id, projection hint, classification, age)`, and lives in the lane's list, not on the vehicle. The hint is the lane coordinates of the vehicle's rearmost corner, so its s, the smaller of its rear corners' s on that lane: followers care about where a vehicle's rear is. There is one copy of s, so lists and vehicles can't drift apart, and sorting and leader search never touch the `SlotMap`. A ghost, a vehicle's indicator, is a record classified as one. The age is how long the vehicle has been in the lane's traffic (see [the longitudinal model](models/longitudinal.md), Relaxation).
- **Classification, joining and leaving** are described in [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md). A vehicle joins a lane when it advances onto it, when its rear axle passes the end of the previous lane, or changes lanes into it.
- **Leaving the network:** a vehicle leaves when its rear axle passes the end of the lane it follows and that lane has no successors. It is queued for removal, as `remove` queues one, and the queue is emptied at the end of the step's motion, every record of it going with it.
- A vehicle on a loop can briefly have two records in the same lane, at two different s values. This is geometrically true and needs no special handling.
- Records are updated every step: each is re-projected from its hint, cleared records are dropped, and order is restored by insertion sort, which is linear on nearly sorted input.
- Multi-lane membership is the general form of SUMO's "shadow lane" for a vehicle partway through a lane change.

## Timesteps and update pipeline

The simulation steps by a fixed time, set when it is made (`Config`): 1/30 s by default, which halves the cost of a second of traffic against 1/60 s, and still moves a vehicle at 50 km/h under half a metre a step. `Simulation::step()` takes one step; `step_variable(dt)` takes as many as fit in a frame's `dt`, carrying what is left over, as "Fix Your Timestep" has it. A fixed step makes a run the same whatever the frame rate, and a slow frame can't destabilise integration or carry a vehicle past a conflict in one step.

| Rate                                 | Runs                                                                                     | Writes                            |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Every `macro_step` steps (3: 10 Hz), first | Junction queues; lane-change desire and gap checks; longitudinal model (IDM+), with barrier decisions | Queues; desire, indicator, lane; acceleration; commitment |
| Every step                           | Motion (front axle along its course, rear axle trailing), occupancy, barriers passed; then signal controllers | Pose, speed; records; commitment; signal states |

The junction queues are brought up to date just before the decisions that read them, since nothing acts on them between: a vehicle is queued up to a macro step after it can no longer stop comfortably, which the margin up to `SAFE_BRAKING` covers (see Conflicts, merging and junctions). Signals step every step, as their times are counted in steps, and cost nothing.

Each update flows strictly from state to derived state to decisions, then applies the decisions: decisions are computed from a read-only view of the state, so they could parallelise per vehicle, and then written back.

**UI:** the UI draws vehicles between where they were before the last step and where they are, by how far into the next step the frame has reached (`Simulation::alpha`), so they don't jump a step at a time when frames and steps don't line up. They are drawn a step behind.

## Vehicle kinematics

Vehicles move by a kinematic bicycle model driven by where the front axle goes rather than by a steering angle: the front axle is placed on the vehicle's course each step, and the rear axle trails it, as a car's rear wheels follow its front wheels. World pose is the source of truth for the rear axle; the front axle's place is along its path. The 2022 prototype moved its vehicles the same way.

- **Course:** the lane it follows, or, during a lane change, an offset from it along a quintic (see [Lane changes](models/lane-change.md)), with each corner of the lanes' polylines rounded so that its direction doesn't jump at a vertex: within up to 2 m of a vertex, half the shorter of the segments either side, the course is the quadratic Bézier from that far before the vertex to that far after, its control point the vertex, each point at its own offset. That needs no square roots, and, symmetric about the vertex, is parameterised all but evenly by distance. Junction connectors are quintics, so a turn eases in.
- **Trailing:** a rear axle can't slide sideways, so it moves along the body, and the path it traces is the tractrix of the front axle's: on a bend of radius `R` it runs `R − √(R² − L²)` inside the front for a wheelbase `L`, as road design's swept paths have it. Each step it moves along the mean of its heading before and, near enough, after, to a wheelbase behind the front axle: second order in the step, with one square root. Stored as a unit vector, the heading needs no trigonometry.
- **Steering:** none is stored or limited: the front axle follows its course however tight it is. Lane changes are planned no tighter than a 4 m radius from rest, about a car's at full lock, easing to 7 m by 2.5 m/s, and the inspector shows the curvature of the vehicle's path. Changing lanes, the front axle goes less far along its lane per step, by `√(1 + slope²)` for its course's slope there, so it moves at its speed however steep the change.
- **Staying in lane:** outside a lane change, the front axle is on its lane's course and the rear axle follows deterministically, so a body's sweep is known in advance: compilation checks it against every connector (see Arms), and classification predicts a vehicle's course the same way it moves (see [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md)). It isn't checked at runtime, which would cost a check per vehicle per neighbour.
- **Swinging wide** (**Planned**): on a tight turn, drivers swing wide so their rear wheels don't cut the corner. A connector's quintic would be pushed out from each bend by a share of how far a rear axle would cut inside the front's course there, `√(R² + L²) − R`, from its exact curvature, before it is sampled, fading to nothing at its ends, where it doesn't bend; its width and sweep check then follow the line driven. Pushing the sampled polyline instead was tried and turned noise in its estimated curvature into kinks that folded tight turns.

## Models

The models are apart from the core, each behind a module boundary and replaced by editing it, each with its own doc:

- [**Longitudinal model**](models/longitudinal.md): IDM+ (Schakel, van Arem and Netten, 2010), with the Improved IDM above the desired speed, LMRS's relaxation after a lane change, acceleration noise, finishing a stop, lower limits ahead and friction beside congested lanes. Every acceleration is the running minimum of its terms.
- [**Lane-change model**](models/lane-change.md): LMRS (Schakel, Knoop and van Arem, 2012), with relaxation, synchronisation and cooperation, assertiveness and courtesy. It searches nothing itself: a driver's intent asks for its gap to be measured, the core measures it, and the model judges it.
- [**Gap acceptance**](models/gap-acceptance.md): when the head at a barrier commits, from the light, gaps in priority traffic as SUMO judges them from foes' arrival times, impatience, room beyond and arrival order.
- [**Routing**](models/routing.md): random choices at forks, and routes to destinations by Dijkstra's algorithm backward over the lanes, each road's time counted once, with the lane changes each needs.
- [**Signals**](models/signals.md): fixed-time plans.
- [**Driver model**](models/driver.md): each driver's traits, given at insertion beside its vehicle: its desired speed, dawdling, gap to lines and how it changes lanes, and the state they vary with.

Each owns its per-vehicle state, and the core asks it for what it needs (see Simulation state and data layout). Known limitations, oddities and performance worth chasing are collected in [Known issues](known-issues.md). Step times, measured over time, are in [Performance](performance.md).

## Lane membership, lane changes and diverging

How vehicles keep apart while they change lanes, merge and diverge, and when one may pass another, is described in [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md). This section covers the lane-change mechanics it relies on; how drivers decide is the lane-change model's (see [Lane changes](models/lane-change.md)).

- **Where changes happen:** only into a neighbour the network permits changing into, and only where the change would be over, the whole body in the new lane, before the lanes beside the vehicle's path run out, as their neighbour links' stretches say: about its wheelbase, the change's length and the vehicle's length. The lanes beside run out at a junction too: no change starts in a junction or runs into one, as SUMO allows none on internal junction lanes. One that did could stop the vehicle at a conflict partway across, where its search of its old lane could wait on a vehicle that waits on it.
- **The decision is a jump:** when a vehicle changes lanes, the lane it follows switches to the target at once, and it joins the target's occupant list, its ghost becoming its record. Its pose doesn't move; it is simply off-centre on its new lane, and its course brings it onto the centreline.
- **Lateral path:** at the start of a change the vehicle fixes the course of its front axle, a quintic in u = s − s₀ along its new lane, from where its front axle is on its old course, heading its way, so the course doesn't jump: offset t₀ and slope m₀ there, to zero offset and slope at u = L, with no curvature of its own at either end, `t(u) = t₀(1 − 10x³ + 15x⁴ − 6x⁵) + L·m₀(x − 6x³ + 8x⁴ − 3x⁵)` with x = u/L, so steering eases in and out, as automated vehicles' path planners have it. L = max(4 s at its speed, L_min), so peak lateral acceleration is about 1.3 m/s² across a 3.5 m lane at speed. L_min is the shortest it may be, √(6·|t₀|·r), so that its sharpest bend, about 5.8·|t₀| / L², is no tighter than a radius r of 4 m from rest, as drivers steer hard at walking pace, easing to 7 m by 2.5 m/s: about 9 m across a lane from rest, and 12 m once moving. If the vehicle advances mid-change, s₀ shifts back by the old lane's length. A change is complete once its rear axle passes the quintic's end, and no other starts until then.
- **Old side:** the vehicle remembers which side its old lane is on, and maps its path to the old lanes beside it. While it straddles, it runs a second leader search from its record on the old lane, as far as the change's end, so it doesn't run into its old leader; its terms join its own. A vehicle that advances while straddling also joins its old lane's successors, so followers there see it. It straddles while it has a record on the old-side neighbour of the lane it follows, or of the lane before it, just after a section boundary.
- **Passing checks** are made as [Multi-lane car following](multilane-car-following.md) describes, the follower's nose placed at the other vehicle's rearmost corner along its own lane, or, along its old lanes, projected onto its own lane through world space.
- **`change_lane`** executes a change directly, with no safety check, so it can drive a vehicle into another. It is the execute half of the lane-change model's decisions and a testing hook.

- **Searching back:** from a point on a lane, back through that lane's occupants, then the ends of the lanes leading into it, into each only if some vehicle's path through it reaches the lane searched from. Each lane keeps how far beyond its end the paths through it reach: raised whenever a path grows, so never short, and made exact again at each lane-change update. Going back round a loop, the distance only grows, so the search ends there too. On the lane searched from, every vehicle but a ghost counts; on lanes leading in, one counts if that lane is on its path. The first found is the follower if the point is within its horizon. This is how SUMO and Treiber's MOBIL find the follower on the target lane, and `try_insert` finds its follower the same way.
- **Nobody slips between:** the leader search ahead and the follower search behind split each lane at the vehicle's rear, so a vehicle alongside is one or the other, with a gap of zero or less, and refuses the change.
- **Across section boundaries:** when the vehicle advances, its ghost moves to the neighbour of its new lane, or, with none it may change into, the intent is cancelled.

## Conflicts, merging and junctions

Lanes that cross or merge inside an intersection are grouped into a junction, and a junction keeps vehicles on its lanes apart. A lane belongs to at most one junction. A junction keeps vehicles that can no longer stop comfortably from running into each other, and decides which of two goes first once either can no longer give way to the other; when vehicles go before then is for barriers (see Barriers and signals), which defer to it. Its order holds no cycle, so a junction can't deadlock on its own.

- **Conflicts:** a junction stores, for each ordered pair of its lanes, where they conflict, if they do: the kind (merge or cross) and the overlapping s-range on each lane, in a dense `lanes²` table, so a lookup gives "my" range and "theirs" directly. Where each lane's first conflict starts is worked out from the table when the junction is built, and again when a saved network is restored, rather than saved, since the junction's scans ask it constantly. Lanes leading into the same lane merge; branches of a fork, lanes one after the other and neighbours never conflict, since the multi-lane following model keeps vehicles on them apart; any other pair whose outlines overlap crosses. Each connector is one lane; one of several lanes in a row, each the only lane into the next, is one run of them, so branches of a fork are traced back to the first lane of each in the junction, and the lanes of one run never conflict.
- **Finding overlaps:** each segment's piece of a lane's outline is a convex quadrilateral, and every pair of pieces from two lanes is clipped one against the other (Sutherland–Hodgman). Each lane's s-range runs from the first overlap to the last. An overlap thinner on average than rounding to the grid leaves doesn't count, so lanes touching along an edge don't conflict. A merge whose outlines don't overlap gets the lanes' ends.
- **One queue per junction,** part of the state and of snapshots: the vehicles there that can no longer stop comfortably short of their first conflict, in an order, and the lanes vehicles not yet queued are **approaching** it along. Two queued vehicles whose ways through the junction conflict are a **pair**, their conflict running from the first start to the last end of every conflict between a lane of one's way and a lane of the other's, measured along each one's path, a vehicle's way being the junction's lanes on its path and any it is leaving behind. A pair is **decided** once either can no longer give way to the other comfortably: once the term the junction would give it for the other (see Keeping apart) brakes it harder than its comfortable deceleration, or its front is already in their conflict. A vehicle held back before their conflict by a barrier it hasn't committed to will stop there, as at a red light, so needs no braking. Of a decided pair, the lower in the queue gives way to the higher. Until a pair is decided, the order is only a tiebreak, and neither holds the other back, so vehicles still able to stop comfortably, and crossing streams, aren't slowed by order alone.
- **Queue invariants:** a junction can't deadlock on its own, so long as two things hold, and the vehicle at the top of its queue can always move.
  1. The order agrees with every decided pair and with every vehicle following another: below one whose body is ahead on its path, on a lane it is headed along or leaving, or, at a fork, overlapping a branch beside its own until it has diverged, as car following has it. The queue is a topological order of those, so it holds no cycle.
  2. No vehicle has entered a conflict with one above it in the queue that hasn't cleared it.

  The flood tests check that a queued vehicle following another on one lane is below it, and the second, every tenth of a second, as they check that bodies don't overlap, the second for crossing conflicts but for a vehicle following another the same general way, which the junction allows; a cycle shows as a lockup. A stress test, ignored by default as it takes minutes, runs 680 busy intersections, and 120 busy grids of them, for a quarter of an hour each, drivers varied, checking both: signalised crossroads, square and oblique, one to three lanes each way, turning on red or with a keep-clear zone; priority crossroads and T-junctions, with give-way and stop signs; all-way stops; and 4 by 4 grids of signalised and priority crossroads 100 m apart, one and two lanes each way. A grid may gridlock, a queue backing up from one crossroads round through others to itself: a junction whose vehicles all stand still counts as gridlocked, not locked up, if meanwhile one of them had no room on the lane it leaves by, and gridlocks are counted and printed rather than failed.

  Barriers defer to the order, so they can't close a cycle through it: a gap check passes over a vehicle the junction has decided goes after its own, and everyone behind it on that way in, since none of them can reach the conflict first, and goes only behind one that can no longer give way to it; and room beyond counts a body only on its own way, as the junction does (see [Gap acceptance](models/gap-acceptance.md)). Where a barrier waits on a vehicle its own isn't yet decided with, the pair is decided once either reaches its point of no return. That rests on a turner's waiting line coming before its first conflict with the traffic it gives way to, as compilation places it: an oncoming vehicle reaches its conflict with the turn before it could be held up behind anything the turner blocks, so decides their pair. Where geometry puts two turners waiting in each other's way, as on the 60° crossroads, each is in their conflict, so their pair is decided, and the line of the higher defers to the order. Whether a vehicle may enter a junction it can't clear, or block traffic while it waits, is the barriers' and the drivers' business (see [Gap acceptance](models/gap-acceptance.md), Room beyond and Giving way to vehicles inside), as real drivers often do. The UI's turner cycle scenario sets up a cross-street car behind a turner at its waiting point.
- **Arriving:** a vehicle arrives at a junction once a lane of the junction is on its path and no other junction it hasn't entered lies before it, so when its path is extended or rebuilt, or it enters a junction. It adds the lanes of its path up to the first of the junction's lanes with a conflict to those the junction is approached along. The junction keeps no list of who is approaching, only where. A barrier before the junction on its path that it hasn't committed to holds it back too: it arrives once it commits, so vehicles waiting at a red light or a stop sign aren't approaching.
- **Joining:** at each macro step, before the decisions, the junction scans back from the start of the conflict on each of its lanes being approached, along that lane's occupants and the lanes leading in that it is approached along, passing over ghosts, queued vehicles and vehicles whose path has left the way. At the first other vehicle: one headed onto the lane joins the bottom of the queue once it can no longer stop comfortably before the conflict, or its front is past its start, and the scan goes on behind it; one that can still stop, or is headed elsewhere in the junction, stops the scan. So nobody joins above a vehicle ahead of it on its lane that might still go in, which has to be enforced, since IDM only roughly keeps a follower able to stop. Lanes a scan finds no one on, nor beyond, stop being approached.
- **Ordering:** at each macro step, after joining, the junction walks the pairs of its queue, each vehicle's from the nearest above it up, as the queue was ordered when the walk began, each pair seen once by where its two are now. Once either can no longer give way comfortably, a vehicle that needs more than 0.5 m/s² more braking to give way to the one above it than that one would need to give way to it is lifted above it, unless that would close a cycle: with every vehicle between them that it must stay below, above the one it passes and every vehicle between them that must stay below that, by Pearce and Kelly's dynamic topological sort (2006), so only those move, among the places they had, and every other pair keeps its order. One following the other is never lifted above it. The margin keeps two needing about the same braking from swapping back and forth. Lifting a vehicle over the nearest first keeps a further lift from being refused for a pair not yet visited; one refused is tried again at the next update. So the order settles into the order vehicles must go through their conflicts, and holds once the one above can no longer stop.
- **Releasing:** at each macro step, a queued vehicle that could now stop comfortably a car length short of where it joins is released, unless one queued after it is behind it on its way in. The car length is the hysteresis against joining.
- **Leaving:** a vehicle leaves the queue once no lane of the junction is on its path and it has no record on any of them, or when it is removed.
- **Keeping apart:** at each longitudinal update, the junction gives each queued vehicle a term from each vehicle above it that it is a decided pair with, for every conflict between a lane of its path in the junction and a lane of the junction's. A body on a lane its vehicle isn't headed along counts only where it is left behind, as the vehicle leaves that lane or an earlier lane of its own connector; one that overlaps a lane beside its own, as branches of a fork do, is in the way only where its own lanes are. For each: until the one above has cleared the conflict on its lane, the gentlest of three ways of keeping out of its way, each safe on its own:
  - **Stopping short** of the conflict.
  - **Following its progress:** a virtual vehicle at the speed of the one ahead, as far past the start of the follower's conflict as that one's rear is past the end of its own, so it reaches that start just as the other clears: virtual vehicle mapping (Uno et al., 1999). Crossing streams roll through one behind another rather than stopping at the line.
  - **Following it,** while it moves the same general way as the follower's conflict runs, now and as it leaves its own: its nearest corner along that direction, at its speed along it. A convex body is nowhere nearer along a direction than its nearest corner, and checking its heading as it leaves too stops a vehicle turning across from leading the follower in and then swinging back.

  The same terms decide pairs, so whether a vehicle can still give way is judged as it would give way. Between deciding at comfortable braking and `SAFE_BRAKING` lies the margin for two vehicles deciding at once, or one a macro step late, and on to IDM's limit, `MAX_BRAKING` (9 m/s²), the backstop's. A follower past the start of its conflict is held back all the same: at a merge, dropping back is the only way out.
- **Barriers inside a junction:** a turner waiting inside for a gap is waiting by design. Its give-way line is partway along its connector, and it waits there in the queue as any vehicle does. While it waits, its barrier holds it back before the traffic it gives way to, so its pairs with that traffic aren't decided by it, and it holds nobody up there; taking its gap, it gives way to whatever has already decided above it, as its gap check, which goes only behind vehicles that can no longer give way to it, expects. A vehicle waits inside only once it is in, on one of the junction's lanes or committed to a barrier on one: at its stop line it isn't, though its line is on the same connector as a turner's waiting line further on.
- **Lane changes:** none start in a junction or run into one, so a vehicle is never partway across a junction's lanes. A queued vehicle that changes lanes on its way in keeps its place.
- **Deadlock:** the order holds no cycle, and barriers defer to it, so the vehicle at the top of a queue moves, or waits on a light, a gap that will come, or room beyond. A vehicle can still be blocked by traffic beyond the junction: **gridlock** is allowed. A queued vehicle stopped more than a car length short of its conflict is released, so it holds nobody up while it waits.
- **Cost:** per junction, O(q²) pairs at 10 Hz for q queued vehicles, tens at most, each pair's conflict and the terms each would take worked out to decide it, twice, once to order the queue and once for the terms; a search over a stretch of the queue for each lift; and a scan past the queued vehicles on each lane approached.

**Off the beaten path:** SUMO resolves junctions per link, from foe matrices and each vehicle's announced arrival time, with no global order. One order per junction, deciding which of two goes first through their conflict only once one can no longer give way, and kept free of cycles, is closer to the precedence orders of multi-robot coordination on fixed paths (Pecora and others), which revise precedence online to avoid deadlock, and to Dresner and Stone's AIM reservations and A/B Street's intersection queues.

## Merges

Where lanes outside junctions that were apart start to overlap, as at a lane drop or where two roads' lanes meet one, a merge orders the vehicles approaching and opens gaps between them before they meet. Inside the merge, vehicles keep apart by following each other, which keeps them from colliding but not from arriving side by side and braking hard. A merge's only job is to stop that. Merges are kept apart from junctions, in their own module: a junction orders vehicles once they can no longer stop, and forbids lane changes; a merge orders them early and lets them change lanes on the way in.

- **Which lanes:** a group of lanes outside junctions that overlap one another, joined pair by pair, where the two lanes of each pair have no predecessor in common and no two of their predecessors overlap: the boundary where disjoint lanes start merging. Its merge point is the start of its lanes. Merges are derived when the simulation is built, so they aren't part of a snapshot.
- **The order:** the vehicles approaching, by the distance from each one's rear to the merge point, nearest first. Nothing is stored: at each longitudinal update the order is worked out by walking back through the lanes leading in, as far as a path reaches, and marching back along them all at once, as the merge step of a merge sort does, taking the nearest of the lanes' next vehicles each time. A lane's vehicles count if they follow it and their path leads into the merge, so a vehicle is in the order once a merge lane is on its path, well before the merge, and leaves it once it is on a merge lane, where its followers find it through their records. A vehicle that pulls ahead goes first at once, as on the road; close to the merge point the terms keep vehicles from being level, so the order doesn't flicker.
- **By distance, not estimated arrival:** a vehicle's leader in the order is then never further from the merge point, which the term's slack covers. By estimated arrival, a fast vehicle could come before a slow one well ahead of it, which would brake as hard as it could to fall in behind a leader behind it, and a stopped vehicle's arrival never comes, so a queue locked up. A fast vehicle instead gives way to a slower one just ahead, gently, as drivers do.
- **The term:** each vehicle F whose front hasn't reached the merge point follows the one before it in the order, L, by IDM, as a virtual vehicle on F's lane as far from the merge point (Uno et al., 1999), with the headway it will keep just after merging, LMRS's `T_min/T_max` of its own. Slack is added to the gap, enough for F to be level with L at first, shrinking linearly to nothing at the merge point from 8 s before it at F's speed, or 50 m if that is further: so the gap opens gradually as both draw near, and queues go through by turns, each vehicle moving off as soon as its leader is clear. With less than 50 m, crawling queues reach the merge point side by side and go through by turns from rest, a third less getting through. The term never brakes harder than comfortably: before the merge point the lanes are apart, and inside the merge, following keeps vehicles apart however they arrive.
- **Cost:** at 10 Hz, a march along the occupants of the lanes leading into each merge, and one term per vehicle in it.

**Off the beaten path:** SUMO orders vehicles at its `zipper` junctions by estimated arrival, and Treiber's simulator treats an on-ramp as mandatory MOBIL lane changes. Ordering by distance and following a virtual leader is the usual pairing in cooperative merging (Uno et al., 1999); the shrinking slack is this simulator's own.

## Barriers and signals

Stop lines, give-way lines, signals and the starts of keep-clear zones are all barriers: a point on a lane, with a kind. A barrier may also carry its line in world space, as a half plane, where the line isn't square across the lane, as an oblique stop line isn't: its point on the lane is then where no vehicle crossing the line along the lane could reach it sooner, the line's crossing of the centreline set back by the lane's half-width times the tangent of its skew, since no vehicle on the lane is wider than the lane. A vehicle's gap to it is the greater of its gap to that point and how far its nearest corner is short of the line: the point is safe, the line exact, so a narrow vehicle stops closer to an oblique line than a wide one. Order, shielding and passing go by the point. A vehicle treats the first barrier on its path that it hasn't committed to as a stationary leader at the line, with its own gap to lines as the minimum gap, so it stops a little short of the line, until it commits to crossing; from then on the barrier isn't there for it. The gap is drawn afresh each time a vehicle heads for a new barrier, from its own seeded generator, uniformly within its driver's `line_gap_spread` of its `line_gap` (see [the driver model](models/driver.md)): about 1 m, within 0.5 m either way for a car, so a row of vehicles at a line doesn't stand in perfect step. This is how Treiber's simulator treats a red light, and how SUMO's link states hold vehicles at a junction. **Built:** signals, give-way and stop lines, all-way stops and keep-clear zones, commitment, room beyond and holding back arrival at a junction. **Planned:** tram stops.

- **Commitment** is a count: how many of the barriers ahead of its rear axle on its path it has committed to, in path order. Passing a barrier takes one off, and a lane change or a new path wipes it, since its barriers are then others. A count rather than a flag lets it be committed to several at once, such as a stop line and a waiting point just beyond it, though it commits to at most one at each update.
- **Only the head decides:** the leader search finds a barrier as it finds a leader, and an in-lane leader before it that hasn't committed to it shields it, as it would a vehicle beyond: that leader will stop there, so the follower stops behind it anyway. A leader that has committed to it, the barrier among the first of its count ahead of its rear axle, shields nothing, so the follower sees the barrier and decides for itself when it gets there. So the head for a barrier is the first vehicle on its way that hasn't committed to it, with no queue stored. Each barrier found carries its rank among those ahead of the vehicle, so one beyond a barrier it can't see, shielded, isn't decided out of turn. A vehicle's own committed barriers are found too, for release (below). The search stops at the first barrier it hasn't committed to, since nothing beyond can matter while it may stop there, unless that barrier may let it pass for now, a green light or a keep-clear zone, when it goes on to the leaders beyond and stops at the next. So a vehicle held at a line doesn't see a leader just past it, and one committing sees beyond from the next update.

- **When it commits** is the gap-acceptance model's to decide, at each longitudinal update, once the search is done, for the head's barrier alone, from a read-only view of the state: a signal on green once the vehicle can no longer stop comfortably, a give-way or stop line once there is a gap, an all-way stop in arrival order, a keep-clear zone once there is room beyond (see [Gap acceptance](models/gap-acceptance.md)). It answers commit, pass for now (only at a green light or a keep-clear zone), yield to a vehicle, or stop, and the core carries the answer out, holding a vehicle that yields or stops as a stationary leader at the line, at its gap to lines.
- **Coming to rest** at a give-way or stop line, or a red light it may turn on, as the head with the barrier first ahead, at rest within half a metre further back than its gap to lines, not further back behind a vehicle headed elsewhere, records the step it happens, until it passes the line, commits, or changes lanes: a stop line, and turning on red, need it before a vehicle may take a gap, vehicles of equal priority take turns by it, and it tells how long a driver has waited (see [Gap acceptance](models/gap-acceptance.md), Impatience).
- **Passing:** each step, a committed vehicle takes one off its count for each barrier its rear axle passes.
- **Release:** a committed vehicle slowed short of a barrier, by traffic ahead or a junction, may no longer need to go. At a red light, one that can still stop before the line without braking harder than `SAFE_BRAKING` is released from it, and from those after it, and decides afresh, as a driver who could stop does; at a give-way or stop line, or a keep-clear zone, one that has come to rest short of the line is, since the gap it took is stale. On amber a vehicle goes on, one caught in the dilemma zone, and a turner waiting past its line for a gap, which comes as the oncoming traffic stops, unless it has come to rest short of the line, held there by traffic ahead, when it is released as at a stop line: a driver standing at the line doesn't move off into an amber light. Before, one at rest just short of the line could pull away on amber behind its leader and cross on red.

**Signals** run fixed-time plans (see [Signals](models/signals.md)); the movements' lights are one flat array in the state, which signal barriers read.

## Demand (Planned)

The `demand` crate places vehicles in the simulation and gives them routes, through the external API; the core knows nothing of it.

- **Flows:** a flow per entry lane, in vehicles per hour, with exponential headways (Poisson arrivals) from a seeded generator, as SUMO's flows can have. A vehicle that finds no room waits, in order, at its entry, and is inserted with `try_insert` as soon as there is room, so demand above capacity queues outside the network rather than vanishing, as SUMO's insertion backlog does.
- **Origin–destination demand:** flows from entries to destinations named by road ends, each vehicle drawing its destination from its entry's share. Without destinations, routes are random.
- **Closed networks:** a target count, kept by inserting into the largest gap and removing at random, as the UI does now on the ring.
- **Vehicle mix:** each vehicle's class drawn from a mix, then its driver from distributions: a speed factor normal with mean 1 and deviation 0.05, clamped to 0.85–1.15, so nearly every driver on a 50 km/h road wants 45 to 55 km/h: narrower than SUMO's default deviation of 0.1, which spreads them from 40 to 60; assertiveness a uniform number cubed; courtesy and politeness uniform; and its gap to lines about 1 m.
- **Trams** run to a timetable on their routes, a destination each, rather than from flows.
- The UI's spawning moves here, and the UI uses the crate. Demand's generators aren't part of the simulation's state, so a freeze frame holds no demand, and a replay records the vehicles inserted, not how they were drawn.

## Vehicle classes (Planned)

Each vehicle has a class, a small enum: car, truck, bus or tram. A class restricts which lanes the vehicle may use, as SUMO's vehicle classes and lane permissions do, and has a `VehicleSpec` preset, its dimensions and driver parameters from published defaults (OpenTrafficSim's and SUMO's for those vehicle types).

- **Lane use:** each lane has a set of classes allowed on it, a bitmask. A vehicle's path only takes lanes its class may use, its route chooses only among them, and it changes lanes only into them. Classes change nothing else: the leader and follower searches, merges and junctions see every vehicle on a lane, allowed or not, since every body is in the way.
- **Trams** run on tram tracks, lanes only trams may use or shared with general traffic, and never change lanes. A tram follows its track's centreline exactly, its pose set from the lane rather than steered, since rails leave no room to cut a corner. It is drawn articulated, its sections following the track.
- **Tram stops** are barriers that only trams see, with a dwell time: a tram stops at one, waits there for the dwell, then commits. A stop in the road, with no platform between the tram and the lanes beside it, also puts a barrier on each lane beside the track, level with the rear of a tram at the stop, which only other classes see and which holds them while a tram dwells there, as Victoria's road rules require.

## External API

Demand lives outside the `sim` crate. The simulation exposes:

- **Making and stepping:** `Simulation::new` or `with_config`, `step`, `step_variable`, `alpha`, `time`; and `step_debug` and `step_variable_debug`, which step the same way and append what the algorithms did to a buffer the caller owns (see Debug instrumentation).
- **Inserting:** `insert(vehicle, driver, at)` places a vehicle, with its driver (see [the driver model](models/driver.md)), on a lane's centreline with no checks, for tests and scripted setups. `try_insert` refuses when there is no safe room: when the new vehicle would overlap another, or it or its new follower would have to brake harder than `SAFE_BRAKING` because of the other; its speed is capped at its new leader's. Its leader and follower come from the same two searches a lane change uses, split at its rear.
- **Destinations:** `add_destination` registers a set of lanes and returns its id, for a route to it (`Route::to`), working out its route table then; `time_to` reads the table, the time from a lane's start and the lane changes it needs.
- **Reading:** a vehicle by key, every vehicle, each lane's occupants, each junction's queue and the lanes it is approached along, the network, each signal movement's light (`light`) and each vehicle's commitment. `vehicle_at` picks the vehicle nearest a point, within a tolerance, by a linear scan.
- **Changing:** `remove`, which queues a vehicle for removal, carried out first thing in the next step, as vehicles leaving the network are removed; `change_lane`; `cap_speed`, under which a vehicle brakes at `SAFE_BRAKING` while above the cap and eases up to it from below, `hold` (a cap of 0) and `release`. Capped vehicles are kept in a short list, not as a field on every vehicle, and the cap is one more acceleration term.
- **Snapshot and restore** the whole state, network included, for freeze frames: `state` lends the `SimState`, to clone or, with the `serde` feature, serialize, and `restore` builds a simulation from one. A restored simulation continues bit for bit until it inserts a vehicle into a slot freed before the snapshot: `SlotMap` rebuilds its free list in index order, so that vehicle's id, and the update order after it, can differ.
- **Heterogeneity** comes from inserting vehicles with varied attributes and drivers with varied traits, each driver with a seed for its own generator. Distributions are the caller's concern, not the core's.

## Validation (Planned)

Visual realism comes first, but traffic flow mustn't break down in ways real traffic wouldn't, so a headless harness measures the simulator's aggregate behaviour against published values. It is a crate of its own, `validation`, depending on `sim`, `network` and `demand`, run on demand rather than as a test, and printing each measure beside its reference.

- **Measuring:** virtual detectors outside the core, counting the vehicles crossing a point and their speeds, and timing vehicles between points, from the vehicles' poses after each step, on scenarios built for each measure.
- **What it measures, against what:**
  - A freeway lane's capacity: about 2,400 veh/h at 120 km/h (Highway Capacity Manual).
  - A merged lane's throughput: 2,000–2,400 veh/h (see [Known issues](known-issues.md), Merge capacity).
  - Capacity drop once a bottleneck breaks down: discharge a few percent to about 20% below the flow before it (Hall and Agyemang-Duah, 1991; later studies).
  - Stop-and-go waves: travelling upstream at about 15 km/h, whatever the traffic (Treiber and Kesting), and forming on a ring at Sugiyama's density.
  - Jam density: about 130–150 vehicles per km per lane.
  - Saturation flow at signals: about 1,900 veh/h per lane, about 1.9 s between vehicles once a queue is moving, with about 2 s of start-up lost time (Highway Capacity Manual).
  - Gap acceptance at give-way and stop signs: critical headways of about 4–7 s by movement, and follow-up headways of about 2–3.5 s (Highway Capacity Manual).
- **Reading it:** each measure prints with its reference range, so a change that moves a capacity shows up; ranges are references, not pass or fail. A measure far outside its range is a bug or a model fault to look into, and the reason goes into this document.

**Suggestion:** the same scenarios and demand run on the old simulator, on the `master` branch, side by side with this one (see [Comparing with the old simulator](legacy-comparison.md)).

**Suggestion:** later, the same scenarios exported to SUMO, at road level through netconvert's plain XML, as a second reference for network-wide behaviour such as spillback and gridlock.

## Debug instrumentation

Algorithms report what they are doing through a debug sink that costs nothing when debug output is off. Inside the simulation, the update pipeline is generic over a crate-private `DebugSink`, passed down to each algorithm, and events are passed as closures, `sink.record(|| …)`, so with the zero-sized no-op sink they compile away entirely. `Simulation` itself isn't generic: `step` runs the pipeline with the no-op sink, and `step_debug` with one that appends each event to a `Vec<DebugEvent>` the caller owns, so it is reused rather than allocated each frame. Both versions of the pipeline are compiled in the `sim` crate. A feature flag would have needed a separate build for debugging.

The debug view shows what the simulation actually used, never a recomputation. Events are grouped by the update that produces them, and the caller applies them in order to what it keeps:

- **Resets:** each update first records a reset for its group, "recompute accelerations" and "recompute motion" for everyone, and "recompute decision" per vehicle, which clears that group's earlier events; a removed vehicle's events go with a "vehicle removed" event, recorded when the removal is carried out, in a step. The UI applies a frame's events once its steps return, so it always shows the last complete set of each group.
- **Acceleration terms:** each term is its own event, the free-road term and each leader, junction, merge, cap and barrier term, and each term a model adds, which the model names, as the lane-change model names synchronising and making room, so the debug view needs to know nothing of the model. Every input to the acceleration is recorded, so the binding term is the one equal to the vehicle's acceleration, and one whose terms don't reach it shows an event is missing.
- **Also recorded:** where each front axle was placed, passing checks, with the follower's predicted pose, the other vehicle's nearer rear corner and the gap between them, and what each model weighed up for a vehicle, such as each side the lane-change model weighed up a change to, which a model describes as named fields, numbers, flags, text or nothing, so the debug view shows them knowing nothing of the model. Each barrier decision, with the gap to the line and what the vehicle did: committed, passed for now, or held, with its term, and the vehicle it gives way to if it is held for one: the first with priority it found no gap before or behind, or one already in the junction it gives way to. **Planned:** each gap check, with every priority vehicle found, its reach time and the yielding vehicle's time to clear; and each signal change.

## UI

The `ui` crate renders the simulation with egui's painter and offers two views.

- **Pretty view:** road and junction surfaces, the selected vehicle's path over them, a thick dashed line along the lanes it knows it will take, its dashes flowing slowly the way it goes, lane markings, stop lines and turn arrows from the render geometry, vehicles as oriented shapes drawn between steps, and indicators. Each signal barrier's lane is drawn from the barrier on in its movement's light's colour while green or amber, and left out while red, as the old prototype's visualiser drew them; a far-side turn goes on through the junction in white from its give-way line, open but giving way; and keep-clear zones as yellow hatched boxes.
- **Debug view:** layers for the selected vehicle's path, under everything else, lane outlines (their ends dashed), centrelines, s ticks, neighbours, occupants, vehicles with their axles, lane-change paths, leaders and merge orders, passing checks, barriers across their lanes in their lights' colours, and (**Planned**) gap checks. Clicking a vehicle opens an inspector with its state, vehicles named by their slot's index alone, each vehicle it gives way to or its acceleration terms name in its own colour, as both views then draw it, with every other vehicle white, what its models last weighed up, a table for each model with a row for each field, its acceleration terms, its commitment and its last barrier decisions, and its route: random, or the destination's lanes, with the time to them and the lane changes it needs from its lane's start.
- **Controls:** the scenario to start on, named on the command line (`cargo run -p ui -- signals-2`; an unknown name lists them); pause (space), single step, speed multiplier (keys 1–4 for 1×, 2×, 5× and 20×), camera pan and zoom, scenario choice, traffic (a target count on the ring, a flow per entry lane elsewhere, each vehicle routed to an exit it can reach, drawn at random, the lanes leading out at a road's end, or, unticked, choosing at random at each fork; and (**Planned**) origin–destination demand), lane changes for the selected vehicle, and saving a replay or a freeze frame. Beside them, the average time the last 30 fixed steps took to simulate, with the UI's debug recording, and its share of the step each simulates: per step, so it doesn't vary with the speed, and drawing doesn't count. The crossroads' roads reach 300 m from the centre. Signalised crossroads are a scenario, square with one to three lanes each way and with the roads crossing at 60°, their stop lines skewed along the other road; so are all-way stops, with one and two lanes each way; priority crossroads, the east–west road with priority, and T-junctions, the road from the south without it, each with give-way and with stop signs, one lane each way on both roads or one on the minor road and two on the major, and crossroads giving way with two on each; a signalised crossroads of two lanes each way with a keep-clear zone 45 to 60 m back on the way in from the west; one turning left on red; and a grid of 4 by 4 crossroads 100 m apart, of roads with two lanes each way, alternately signalised, each phase lasting 25 to 35 s so that neighbouring signals drift in and out of step, and controlled by priority, the east–west road having it, each crossroads connected and its arms fitted as a lone crossroads of the same roads is. A scenario may also be a freeze frame, built by script in `traffic_network::frames` and restored, opening paused, with no traffic added: the turner cycle shows a cross-street car stopped behind a turner at its waiting point, in the way of its turn (see Queue invariants). **Planned scenarios:** a Melbourne crossroads with tram tracks and hook turns.

**Suggestion:** if tessellation becomes the bottleneck at high vehicle counts, draw vehicles and roads through a custom wgpu paint callback with instancing, keeping egui for overlays and panels.

## Hard parts

The hardest problems are junction geometry and dense merging. Both have defeated simpler simulators.

1. Generating junction geometry: connector curves and junction surfaces.
2. Choosing lanes several links ahead of a diverge, and mandatory lane changes in dense traffic.
3. Keeping vehicles on the lane geometry without visible snapping.
4. Inserting vehicles without visible pops.
5. Judging realism by eye, with no metric to check against.

Open questions, known limitations and oddities are collected in [Known issues](known-issues.md).

## Milestones

Each milestone is a demo that can be finished on its own; anything after it is optional.

1. Workspace skeleton: one straight lane, IDM, kinematics, the pretty and debug views. **Done.**
2. A ring road showing emergent stop-and-go waves, as in the Sugiyama experiment. **Done.**
3. A multi-lane freeway with continuous lane changes and multi-lane membership, first with MOBIL, now LMRS. `sim/tests/scenario.rs` runs a three-lane ring headless as a stress test. **Done.**
4. An on-ramp merge and an off-ramp diverge. Merges, with zipper or early merging, the diverge's car following, and taking the off-ramp by destination routing and its route desire. **Done.**
5. A single signalised intersection with barriers, a fixed signal plan and unprotected turns. **Done.**
6. Hook turns and trams: tram tracks in the middle of the road, tram stops, and vehicle classes restricting lane use; and swinging wide before tight turns (see Vehicle kinematics).
7. Unsignalised intersections: priority roads with give-way and stop signs, all-way stops and keep-clear zones; and turning on red at signals. **Done.**
8. A procedural grid with destination and random routing, origin–destination demand from the `demand` crate, and a mix of vehicle classes. The grid, random and destination routing are done.
9. The validation harness, measuring capacities, waves, saturation flows and gaps against published values.

## References and prior art

These are the established approaches this design borrows from or deliberately departs from.

- Treiber and Kesting, _Traffic Flow Dynamics_: the textbook on IDM, the Improved IDM and MOBIL, by their authors; and Treiber and Kesting (2017), on IDM with acceleration noise.
- Schakel, van Arem and Netten (2010), IDM+; and Schakel, Knoop and van Arem (2012), LMRS, with relaxation and synchronization, as OpenTrafficSim implements it.
- [traffic-simulation.de](https://traffic-simulation.de): Treiber's browser simulator, built on IDM and MOBIL with ramps and roundabouts. Readable source.
- [SUMO](https://eclipse.dev/sumo/): the reference lane-based microsimulator. See LC2013, shadow lanes, internal junction lanes, zipper junctions and "best lanes".
- Uno et al. (1999), virtual vehicle mapping for merging; Hidas (2002, 2005), cooperative lane changing; Jang and Cassidy (2012), the friction effect beside congested lanes.
- [A/B Street](https://github.com/a-b-street/abstreet): an open-source Rust traffic simulator with detailed write-ups on junction geometry and gridlock.
- [ASAM OpenDRIVE](https://www.asam.net/standards/detail/opendrive/): road description with s/t lane coordinates and explicit junction connections.
- [Fix Your Timestep](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/): fixed timesteps with interpolated rendering.
- Hall and Agyemang-Duah (1991), capacity drop at freeway bottlenecks; Sugiyama et al. (2008), jams forming on a ring with no bottleneck.
- Dresner and Stone (2008), autonomous intersection management by reservation; the Highway Capacity Manual (TRB), critical gaps at unsignalised intersections; and ITE's change interval, for amber and all-red times.
- Swept-path analysis in road design (AASHTO, Austroads), whose tractrix of a vehicle's rear axle behind its front the kinematics follows; and quintic paths for lane changes in automated-vehicle planning.
